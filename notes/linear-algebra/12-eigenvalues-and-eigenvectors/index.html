12. Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

January 1, 2024 · 2 min · 367 words · Your Name

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors describe how a matrix stretches and rotates; they underlie diagonalization, stability of dynamical systems, and principal component analysis.

Definitions#

  • Eigenvalue equation: $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}$ for $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$.
  • Characteristic equation: $p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = 0$; roots are eigenvalues.
  • Eigenspace $E_\lambda$: $\text{Nul}(A - \lambda I)$ (all eigenvectors for $\lambda$ plus $\mathbf{0}$).

Trace, determinant, and eigenvalues#

For $n \times n$ matrix $A$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$:

  • Trace: $\text{tr}(A) = \sum_i a_{ii} = \sum_i \lambda_i$.
  • Determinant: $\det(A) = \prod_i \lambda_i$.
  • $A$ is invertible iff $0$ is not an eigenvalue.

Multiplicity and diagonalizability#

  • Algebraic multiplicity (AM): Multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of $p(\lambda)$.
  • Geometric multiplicity (GM): $\dim(E_\lambda) = \dim(\text{Nul}(A - \lambda I))$.
  • Always: $1 \le \text{GM} \le \text{AM}$.
  • Diagonalizable: $A$ has a basis of eigenvectors iff $\text{GM} = \text{AM}$ for every eigenvalue. Then $A = P \Lambda P^{-1}$ with $P = [\mathbf{v}_1 \ \cdots \ \mathbf{v}_n]$ and $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$.
  • Defective: Some $\lambda$ has $\text{GM} < \text{AM}$; $A$ is not diagonalizable (use Jordan form).

Spectral theorem (symmetric matrices)#

If $A = A^T$ (real symmetric), then:

  1. All eigenvalues are real.
  2. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
  3. $A$ is orthogonally diagonalizable: $A = Q \Lambda Q^T$ with $Q^T = Q^{-1}$.

So we can choose an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors—the foundation of PCA and many spectral methods.

Functional identities#

If $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}$:

  • $A^k \mathbf{v} = \lambda^k \mathbf{v}$; $A^k = P \Lambda^k P^{-1}$.
  • $A^{-1}\mathbf{v} = \lambda^{-1} \mathbf{v}$ (if $\lambda \neq 0$).
  • For a polynomial $q$, $q(A)$ has eigenvalues $q(\lambda)$.
  • Matrix exponential: $e^{At} = P e^{\Lambda t} P^{-1} = P ,\text{diag}(e^{\lambda_1 t}, \ldots, e^{\lambda_n t}) P^{-1}$.
  • Spectral radius: $\rho(A) = \max_i |\lambda_i|$; $A^k \to 0$ iff $\rho(A) < 1$.

Cayley–Hamilton theorem#

Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation: $p(A) = \mathbf{0}$. So $A^n$ (and higher powers) can be written as a linear combination of $I, A, \ldots, A^{n-1}$; this can be used to compute $A^{-1}$ or $e^{At}$.

Gerschgorin circle theorem#

Every eigenvalue of $A$ lies in at least one Gerschgorin disc:

$$ D_i = \left{ z \in \mathbb{C} : |z - a_{ii}| \le \sum_{j \neq i} |a_{ij}| \right} $$

(sum of off-diagonal absolute values in row $i$). Useful for quick bounds on eigenvalue locations.
